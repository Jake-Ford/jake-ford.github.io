Home Ownership by Race

2022-05-31 · The persistent gap and its structural origins.

The racial homeownership gap in the United States is one of the most durable and well-documented forms of wealth inequality in the country. As of the most recent data: the white homeownership rate sits around 74%, Black homeownership around 44% — a ~30 percentage point gap that has remained stubbornly persistent for decades, and in some periods has widened since the passage of the Fair Housing Act in 1968.

The Historical Foundation

You can't analyze this gap without grounding it in the deliberate policy choices that created it:

  • FHA loan discrimination: The Federal Housing Administration, from its founding in 1934 through at least the 1960s, systematically denied mortgage insurance in Black neighborhoods and in neighborhoods that were racially mixed. This effectively shut Black Americans out of the suburban housing boom that built the American middle class.

  • HOLC redlining: As detailed in the previous post on this topic, the Home Owners' Loan Corporation created explicit racial maps of "risky" neighborhoods that became the template for private lenders.

  • Restrictive covenants: Private contracts barring the sale of homes to non-white buyers were legal and widely used until Shelley v. Kraemer in 1948, and even after that ruling, often enforced through social and economic pressure.

  • Urban renewal / "Negro removal": Mid-century highway construction and "slum clearance" programs disproportionately demolished Black neighborhoods, displacing homeowners without equivalent replacement.

The Compounding Effect

Homeownership is the primary vehicle of intergenerational wealth transfer in the United States. Missing out on the 1940s–1970s housing appreciation — the period when most of the current housing stock was built and appreciated — means a structural head start for white households and a structural gap for Black households that compounds decade over decade.

Current Barriers

The historical gap is compounded by present-day structural issues:
- Credit score discrimination: Standard credit models don't incorporate rent payment history, which disproportionately disadvantages renters (who skew younger, lower-income, and non-white).
- Down payment requirements: Without inherited wealth, the 20% down payment standard is an enormous barrier.
- Appraisal bias: Documented patterns of lower appraisals for homes in Black neighborhoods, regardless of comparable sales.

The housing gap is not a mystery. It is a policy outcome. The more interesting question is what combination of policies could meaningfully close it — and whether there's political will to try.
