---
title: "Quantifying NIMBYism"
description: "Using zoning data to measure housing obstruction."
---

NIMBY — Not In My Back Yard — is a well-understood cultural phenomenon: the person who supports affordable housing in the abstract and opposes every specific affordable housing development proposed near them. What's less well-understood is the aggregate policy impact of NIMBYism as an institutional force, and whether it's quantifiable.

The short answer: yes, with zoning data and building permit records, you can get pretty close.

## The Data Sources

**Zoning data**: Most major US cities now publish their zoning shapefiles. These show, at the parcel level, what can legally be built where. Single-family-only zones (typically classified as R-1 or R-2) are the primary tool of exclusionary zoning.

**Building permits**: Cities publish permit data, which shows where new housing is actually being built — the gap between what's *permitted by zoning* and what's actually *being built* is interesting.

**American Community Survey**: For outcomes data — housing cost burden, density, affordability indices by neighborhood.

## The Measurement Problem

The tricky part of quantifying NIMBYism is distinguishing between:
1. Areas where zoning genuinely prevents denser development (that would otherwise happen)
2. Areas where the market doesn't want to build denser even if it could
3. Areas where historical project-by-project obstruction has chilled development beyond what zoning alone explains

The best proxies I've found: looking at *upzoned* areas and whether construction activity increased post-upzoning (controlling for market conditions), and examining permit denial rates in areas with active neighborhood associations versus those without.

## What the Data Shows

In most cities I've looked at:
- The majority of residential land is zoned exclusively for single-family housing, often in areas that are geographically central and well-served by transit
- Areas with strong neighborhood associations show higher permit denial rates for multifamily projects, even when the projects are code-compliant
- The supply effect of exclusionary zoning concentrates development pressure in areas that are already dense or have weaker political opposition, often lower-income neighborhoods

## The Policy Upshot

NIMBYism isn't just a cultural annoyance — it has a measurable effect on housing supply, and through housing supply on affordability, neighborhood diversity, and urban form. The recent wave of state-level zoning preemption laws (California's SB 9, Oregon's statewide missing middle reform, Montana's 2023 package) represents one approach to getting around local obstruction. Early evidence on supply effects is promising.
