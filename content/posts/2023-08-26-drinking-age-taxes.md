---
title: "Lower Drinking Ages, Higher Tax Revenues?"
description: "A look at the policy math on alcohol minimum age laws."
---

The US federal minimum drinking age of 21 is, in the context of peer nations, an outlier. Most of the developed world settles between 16–18. The argument for keeping the US at 21 is well-worn: road fatalities dropped following the 1984 National Minimum Drinking Age Act, and the research on adolescent brain development provides additional cover.

But there's a policy conversation that doesn't get enough attention: the fiscal and behavioral externalities of a higher drinking age, and whether they cut the direction proponents claim.

## The Setup

The question I was interested in: do states (or countries) with lower minimum drinking ages collect more in alcohol excise tax revenues, controlling for population and income? The intuition: a higher drinking age doesn't eliminate drinking, it just pushes it underground, untaxed, often into less regulated settings.

## What the Data Suggests

The relationship isn't clean — there are too many confounders (state income levels, proximity to state lines, enforcement culture, overall alcohol consumption norms). But a few things stand out:

- States bordering lower-age jurisdictions (or Canada, with its 18/19 minimums in most provinces) show consistent cross-border purchasing effects.
- The underground / informal market for 18–20 year olds in the US is real and well-documented. That consumption isn't being taxed.
- The comparison to countries with lower minimum ages doesn't show dramatically worse health outcomes on most metrics — and some show better outcomes, possibly because normalized drinking in social contexts creates different behavioral patterns than binge drinking in unsupervised settings.

## Caveats

This is not a call to lower the drinking age. The fatality data from pre-1984 is real. The question is whether the policy instrument is well-targeted, and whether there are smarter approaches (graduated licenses for 18–20 year olds, lower BAC limits, better enforcement of *where* and *how* young people drink) that capture the revenue upside while preserving the safety benefits.

The policy conversation deserves more nuance than it usually gets.
